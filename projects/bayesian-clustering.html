1. The Fundamental Dilemma: Why Guessing \(K\) Breaks Down

Every data scientist who has used clustering in production has encountered the same bottleneck: standard algorithms like \(k\)-means or classic Expectation-Maximization Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) force you to fix the number of clusters \(K\) before seeing the data. In exploratory data analysis, biological taxonomy, or unstructured text mining, \(K\) is almost never known in advance. Real-world semantic data is inherently unbounded, continuous, and multi-layered.

To choose \(K\), standard practitioner workflows resort to semi-automated heuristics. You run an Inertia Elbow sweep, pick an inflection point, and then fine-tune it with localized Silhouette Scores. While convenient, these heuristics suffer from severe drawbacks:

  • Computational Redundancy: You are forced to fit dozens of separate models (e.g., 20 or more independent \(k\)-means runs) just to find a candidate \(K\).
  • Brittleness to Embedding Geometry: In high-dimensional representation spaces, distance metrics warp, causing silhouette scores to prefer small, arbitrary numbers of clusters.
  • Lack of Uncertainty and Structure: Classical heuristics return hard cluster memberships without posterior probabilities, component mixing weights, or parent-child hierarchical trees.

The Non-Parametric Bayesian Alternative: Instead of guessing \(K\), I place an infinite-dimensional prior (a Dirichlet Process) over the mixture components. By setting a generous upper truncation bound (such as \(K=30\)), the prior penalizes redundant clusters, automatically shrinking unneeded component weights to zero in a single unified fit.

2. The Benchmark Dataset & Ground-Truth Semantic Hierarchy

To rigorously test these methods, I extracted a curated subset from the canonical 20 Newsgroups corpus (available via scikit-learn.datasets.fetch_20newsgroups). To examine both fine-grained topic resolution and macro-hierarchy discovery, I selected 9 subcategories partitioned across 3 distinct supercategory families:

  • comp.* (Computer Systems): comp.graphics, comp.os.ms-windows.misc, comp.sys.mac.hardware
  • rec.* (Recreation & Sports): rec.autos, rec.motorcycles, rec.sport.baseball
  • sci.* (Science & Technology): sci.crypt, sci.med, sci.space

To avoid trivial data leakage, I stripped all newsgroup email headers, signatures, and quoting footers during extraction. This ensures models cluster documents based purely on topical semantic content rather than poster names, server routing paths, or institutional domains.

This design gives an objective ground truth: exactly 3 parent clusters at the macro level and 9 leaf clusters at the subcategory level. We can now evaluate whether our models uncover both granular topics and high-level families without supervision.

3. Mathematical Challenges: The Curse of Dimensionality & Likelihood Underflow

To represent raw documents as continuous numerical features, I encoded each post using the sentence-transformers model all-MiniLM-L6-v2, which produces dense 384-dimensional semantic vectors. However, fitting a Gaussian mixture model directly in 384 dimensions is mathematically ill-posed due to two fundamental hurdles:

The Curse of Dimensionality & Distance Concentration

As the dimensionality \(D\) grows large, the volume of the space grows exponentially, and Euclidean distances between data points concentrate. For independent, identically distributed features:

$$\lim_{D \to \infty} \frac{\text{dist}_{\max} - \text{dist}_{\min}}{\text{dist}_{\min}} \to 0$$

In 384 continuous dimensions, almost every point is equidistant from every other point (with normalized cosine distances concentrating tightly around \(\sqrt{2}\)), robbing distance-based clustering algorithms of geometric contrast.

Catastrophic Floating-Point Underflow

The probability density function of a \(D\)-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution is given by:

$$p(x \mid \mu, \Sigma) = (2\pi)^{-\frac{D}{2}} |\Sigma|^{-\frac{1}{2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu)\right)$$

The normalizer prefactor \((2\pi)^{-\frac{D}{2}}\) causes severe numerical collapse. When \(D = 384\):

$$(2\pi)^{-\frac{384}{2}} = (2\pi)^{-192} \approx 10^{-153}$$

In standard 64-bit IEEE floating-point arithmetic, computing this product alongside small exponential distance penalties results in immediate underflow to 0.0. Furthermore, inverting a full \(384 \times 384\) empirical covariance matrix requires \(\mathcal{O}(D^3)\) operations and is almost always singular or ill-conditioned when clusters contain few points.

The Architectural Solution: 12D UMAP Manifold Projection

To overcome both issues, I used umap-learn to project the 384-dimensional Sentence Transformer embeddings down to \(D = 12\) dimensions using a cosine metric. UMAP preserves both local nearest-neighbor graphs and global cluster separation. At \(D=12\), Gaussian components remain well-conditioned, normalizers do not underflow, and spherical covariance constraints (\(\Sigma_k = \sigma_k^2 \mathbf{I}\)) remain numerically robust.

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import umap

# 1. Generate dense continuous sentence representations (D = 384)
embedder = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
raw_embeddings = embedder.encode(cleaned_documents, show_progress_bar=True)

# 2. Non-linear manifold dimension reduction to D = 12 for GMM stability
reducer_12d = umap.UMAP(
    n_components=12,
    metric="cosine",
    n_neighbors=15,
    min_dist=0.1,
    random_state=42,
)
umap_embeddings_12d = reducer_12d.fit_transform(raw_embeddings)

4. The Frequentist Baseline: Yellowbrick Two-Step Heuristic Sweeps

Before implementing Bayesian models, I established a baseline using the conventional two-step heuristic workflow via the yellowbrick library. This approach consists of two distinct stages:

  1. Pass 1 (Distortion Elbow): Train \(k\)-means models across \(K \in [2, 16]\) and identify the point of maximum curvature in the within-cluster sum of squares.
  2. Pass 2 (Silhouette Score Fine-Tuning): Sweep a localized window around the elbow candidate \(K_{\text{elbow}} \pm 3\) to select the \(K\) that maximizes average silhouette width.
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from yellowbrick.cluster import KElbowVisualizer

# Pass 1: Inertia Elbow across K in [2, 16] on 12D UMAP embeddings
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(18, 5))
viz_elbow = KElbowVisualizer(
    KMeans(random_state=42, n_init=10),
    k=(2, 17),
    metric="distortion",
    ax=axes[0],
)
viz_elbow.fit(umap_embeddings_12d)

# Pass 2: Localized Silhouette Score sweep around the candidate elbow
k_candidate = viz_elbow.elbow_value_
viz_silhouette = KElbowVisualizer(
    KMeans(random_state=42, n_init=10),
    k=(max(2, k_candidate - 3), k_candidate + 4),
    metric="silhouette",
    ax=axes[1],
)
viz_silhouette.fit(umap_embeddings_12d)

I also repeated this heuristic workflow on linear PCA embeddings (selecting 31 components to preserve 95% variance). PCA selected \(K=7\), but produced blurry boundaries with significant overlap.

The Core Shortcoming: The two-step heuristic required training 22 separate \(k\)-means models. More importantly, it chose \(K=5\) on UMAP, collapsing the 9 true subtopics into 5 clusters and failing to uncover the macro-hierarchy.

5. Phase 2: Continuous DP-GMM & Hierarchy Discovery in Scikit-Learn

To eliminate the need for heuristic sweeping, I implemented a Dirichlet Process Gaussian Mixture Model (DP-GMM) using scikit-learn's BayesianGaussianMixture. Rather than fixing \(K\), I initialized the model with a high truncation limit of \(K = 30\) and a Dirichlet Process prior on the mixing proportions:

from sklearn.mixture import BayesianGaussianMixture
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster

# Single unified fit with high truncation bound K=30
dpgmm = BayesianGaussianMixture(
    n_components=30,
    weight_concentration_prior_type="dirichlet_process",
    weight_concentration_prior=1.0,
    covariance_type="spherical",
    max_iter=300,
    random_state=42,
)
dpgmm.fit(umap_embeddings_12d)

Using covariance_type="spherical" restricts each cluster's covariance to \(\Sigma_k = \sigma_k^2 \mathbf{I}\). This avoids singular matrix inversions in 12D space while allowing Variational EM to converge in 123 iterations.

Automated Macro-Hierarchy Recovery via Ward Agglomeration

While DP-GMM models fine-grained leaf topics, real-world data often possesses higher-level taxonomy. Rather than training a second model, I extracted the active component centroids (\(\mu_k \in \mathbb{R}^{12}\)) and performed Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering using Ward's minimum-variance criterion:

$$d_{\text{Ward}}(A, B) = \frac{n_A n_B}{n_A + n_B} \|\mu_A - \mu_B\|^2$$ 
# 1. Extract active centroids from DP-GMM
active_centroids = dpgmm.means_[active_indices]

# 2. Build agglomerative tree on active centroids using Ward linkage
Z = linkage(active_centroids, method="ward")

# 3. Cut the tree at 3 macro parent clusters
pred_parent_clusters = fcluster(Z, t=3, criterion="maxclust")
document_parent_labels = pred_parent_clusters[point_cluster_assignments]

This automated hierarchy achieved a Parent V-Measure of 0.5403 and an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.4625, successfully grouping fine subtopics back into their parent families without supervision.

6. Phase 3: Native Stick-Breaking DP-GMM in PyMC with ADVI

To inspect the underlying probability mechanics without relying on closed-source black boxes, I built the Dirichlet Process mixture model from first principles using PyMC and PyTensor.

The Sethuraman Stick-Breaking Process

The constructive definition of the Dirichlet Process (Sethuraman, 1994) models the mixture weights as breaking successive fractions off a unit stick:

$$\alpha \sim \text{Gamma}(1.0, 1.0)$$ $$v_k \sim \text{Beta}(1, \alpha), \quad k = 0, \dots, K-2$$ $$w_0 = v_0, \quad w_k = v_k \prod_{j < k} (1 - v_j), \quad w_{K-1} = \prod_{j < K-1} (1 - v_j)$$

Because \(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w_k = 1\), this ensures a valid probability simplex over an unbounded component space.

The Complete PyMC Model Specification

import pymc as pm
import pytensor.tensor as pt

K = 30
N, D = umap_embeddings_12d.shape

with pm.Model() as pymc_dpgmm:
    # 1. Concentration parameter governing cluster dispersion
    alpha = pm.Gamma("alpha", alpha=1.0, beta=1.0)
    
    # 2. Stick fractions v ~ Beta(1, alpha)
    v = pm.Beta("v", alpha=1.0, beta=alpha, shape=K - 1)
    
    # 3. Deterministic stick-breaking mixture proportions
    one_minus_v = 1.0 - v
    cumprod = pt.cumprod(one_minus_v)
    w_0 = v[0:1]
    w_rest = v[1:] * cumprod[:-1]
    w_last = cumprod[-1:]
    w = pm.Deterministic("w", pt.concatenate([w_0, w_rest, w_last]))
    
    # 4. Component Centroids and Spherical Variances
    x_mean = np.mean(umap_embeddings_12d, axis=0)
    x_std = np.std(umap_embeddings_12d, axis=0)
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=x_mean, sigma=x_std * 2.5, shape=(K, D))
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=2.0, shape=K)
    
    # 5. Spherical Gaussian Mixture Likelihood
    comp_dists = [
        pm.Normal.dist(mu=mu[k], sigma=sigma[k], shape=(D,))
        for k in range(K)
    ]
    pm.Mixture("obs", w=w, comp_dists=comp_dists, observed=umap_embeddings_12d)

Why ADVI Over MCMC (NUTS)?

Exact Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling on mixture distributions suffers from label switching: permuting cluster labels leaves the likelihood unchanged, leading to non-identifiable posterior modes and poor mixing across chains. Instead, I used Automatic Differentiation Variational Inference (ADVI) to fit a mean-field approximation in under 60 seconds.

To break symmetry between the 30 initial components, I initialized the variational centroid parameters with \(k\)-means centers, preventing components from collapsing into the global dataset mean.

# Symmetry-breaking initialization via KMeans centers
kmeans_init = KMeans(n_clusters=K, n_init=1, random_state=42).fit(umap_embeddings_12d)
start_vals = {"mu": kmeans_init.cluster_centers_}

with pymc_dpgmm:
    approx = pm.fit(
        n=2500,
        method="advi",
        start=start_vals,
        random_seed=42,
        progressbar=True,
    )
    trace = approx.sample(draws=500, random_seed=42)

Numerically Stable Posterior Responsibilities (Log-Sum-Exp Trick)

After sampling the posterior trace, assigning documents to clusters requires computing posterior responsibilities \(\gamma_{ik} = p(z_i = k \mid x_i)\). Doing this naively in linear probability space risks dividing zero by zero when Gaussian densities underflow. I solved this by evaluating assignments strictly in log-space:

$$\log p(x_i, z_i = k) = \log w_k - \frac{D}{2}\log(2\pi) - D \log \sigma_k - \frac{\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2}{2\sigma_k^2}$$ $$\log \gamma_{ik} = \log p(x_i, z_i = k) - \text{logsumexp}_{j}\left(\log p(x_i, z_i = j)\right)$$ 
from src.utils.math_utils import logsumexp

def compute_posthoc_cluster_responsibilities(X, trace, threshold=0.01):
    # Extract posterior mean parameters
    post_w = np.mean(trace.posterior["w"].values.reshape(-1, K), axis=0)
    post_mu = np.mean(trace.posterior["mu"].values.reshape(-1, K, D), axis=0)
    post_sigma = np.mean(trace.posterior["sigma"].values.reshape(-1, K), axis=0)
    
    # Prune inactive components (weight < 1%)
    act_idx = np.where(post_w >= threshold)[0]
    act_w, act_mu, act_sigma = post_w[act_idx], post_mu[act_idx], post_sigma[act_idx]
    
    # Evaluate joint log-densities
    diff = X[:, np.newaxis, :] - act_mu[np.newaxis, :, :]  # (N, K_act, D)
    sq_dist = np.sum(diff ** 2, axis=-1)                   # (N, K_act)
    log_gauss = -0.5 * D * np.log(2 * np.pi) - D * np.log(act_sigma) - 0.5 * sq_dist / (act_sigma ** 2)
    log_joint = np.log(act_w) + log_gauss
    
    # Stable normalization via Log-Sum-Exp
    log_resp = log_joint - logsumexp(log_joint, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    cluster_assignments = np.argmax(log_resp, axis=-1)
    return cluster_assignments, act_idx, act_w

PyMC Benchmark Performance: The native PyMC DP-GMM achieved the highest leaf-level clustering quality across the entire benchmark, delivering a V-Measure of 0.6077 and an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.4355.

7. Phase 4: Discrete Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (HDP) in Tomotopy

Both previous phases operated on continuous sentence embeddings. However, text in its raw form is a sequence of discrete tokens. To test whether non-parametric Bayesian methods can discover semantic topics without pre-trained neural networks, I implemented a Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (HDP) using tomotopy.

The Two-Level Dirichlet Hierarchy

Formulated by Teh et al. (2006), HDP extends Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) to an unknown number of topics by linking documents through a shared global base measure:

  • Corpus Base Measure: \(G_0 \sim \text{DP}(\gamma, H)\) defines an infinite pool of shared topics over vocabulary \(H\).
  • Document Distribution: \(G_d \sim \text{DP}(\alpha, G_0)\) samples document-specific mixture proportions over the global topics.
import tomotopy as tp

# 1. Initialize HDP Model with Collapsed Gibbs Sampling
hdp = tp.HDPModel(
    tw=tp.TermWeight.IDF,
    min_cf=3,
    rm_top=5,
    alpha=0.1,
    gamma=1.0,
    initial_k=30,
    seed=42,
)

# 2. Add tokenized documents
for doc_tokens in tokenized_corpus:
    hdp.add_doc(doc_tokens)

# 3. Train via Collapsed Gibbs Sampling (500 iterations)
hdp.burn_in = 100
for i in range(0, 500, 25):
    hdp.train(25)
    print(f"Iteration {i}: Perplexity={hdp.perplexity:.2f}, Live Topics={hdp.live_k}")

Even without neural embeddings or pre-trained language models, HDP achieved a Parent ARI of 0.4329, confirming that discrete non-parametric Bayesian topic models can extract macro-hierarchical structure directly from raw word counts.

9. Practical Engineering Guidelines & Conclusion

This research demonstrates that non-parametric Bayesian clustering is not merely a theoretical curiosity; it offers concrete advantages over classical heuristic sweeps when working with complex, high-dimensional datasets. Here are the core engineering takeaways:

  1. When to Use Yellowbrick Heuristics: Fast exploratory analysis on low-dimensional tabular data when you want an immediate visual intuition and do not need posterior probabilities or hierarchical parent trees.
  2. When to Use Scikit-Learn DP-GMM: Production pipelines where computational speed is paramount. Running Variational EM on 12D continuous embeddings takes less than 2 seconds, and coupling active centroids with Ward linkage provides an automated, multi-scale hierarchical tree.
  3. When to Use Native PyMC DP-GMM: Custom statistical architectures where you require explicit control over concentration priors, isotropic noise scales, or full posterior uncertainty quantification. Initializing ADVI with \(k\)-means centroids breaks permutation symmetry, while the Log-Sum-Exp trick prevents numerical underflow.
  4. When to Use Tomotopy HDP: Pure text environments where neural embeddings are unavailable, too heavy, or when word-level topic interpretability is mandatory.

By moving beyond rigid \(k\)-means heuristics and embracing non-parametric priors, we allow our models to discover the natural granularity of the underlying data.
